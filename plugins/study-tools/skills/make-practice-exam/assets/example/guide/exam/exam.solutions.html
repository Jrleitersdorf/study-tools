The cap is a binding price ceiling, so it creates a shortage. At the lower rent, more people want apartments in the city, so the quantity demanded rises, while landlords find renting out less profitable, so the quantity supplied falls. In the short run supply is inelastic, because buildings already exist, so the shortage starts small. Over the years supply becomes more elastic: landlords convert units to condominiums, skip maintenance and build less, while more people move in to take advantage of low rents, so the gap between quantity demanded and supplied widens. The shortage shows up as waiting lists, worse upkeep and a deadweight loss from apartments that would have been rented at the market rent.

A tax drives a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers keep, so fewer units are traded than at the untaxed equilibrium. Each lost unit is one that a buyer valued more than it cost a seller to produce, so a trade that would have made both better off no longer happens. The surplus from those lost trades goes to nobody, not even to the government, which collects tax only on units that are still sold; that lost surplus is the deadweight loss. When demand is more elastic, buyers cut back more in response to the higher price, so the quantity falls further. More lost trades means a larger deadweight loss triangle.

  • The tax raises the buyers' price above the sellers' price and reduces the quantity traded.
  • The lost trades are ones where willingness to pay exceeded the seller's cost.
  • That lost surplus goes to no one (not tax revenue): the deadweight loss.
  • More elastic demand means a larger drop in quantity, so a larger loss.
